Shifu: the last year and what's ahead

A year of pivots, two hundred doors, and a line from 2020.

Nemath Ahmed August 1, 2026 13 min read

I have not written in quite a while. I have wanted to get back to it for some time, and this is my attempt to start again.

A lot has happened since I last wrote. At the same time, it somehow feels like the opposite.

This is a write-up of that stretch and the lessons I plan to take forward.

To situate things quickly: we worked on dotflo until October 2024, then voice AI for insurance companies until February 2025, then Supamill, a conversational workflow builder that connected your apps and browser and ran multi-step automations from a plain-language request, until September 2025. From September to December, we were deep in home services. Since mid-January 2026, we have been back in health, which is where this story ends up. Spencer and I have been through all of it together.

What a day looked like in pivot hell

There were different days, but the dilemma in my head about whether we were pursuing the right thing remained every day.

There were days when we were knocking on doors and getting great responses that aligned with our hypothesis, and we would come back to the whiteboard with a feeling of being onto something. The next day, those same hypotheses would get broken into pieces, and we would be back at the whiteboard questioning whether we were at square one again.

The only thing that helped me personally was having two anchors every day. Mornings were strength sessions, and evenings were Muay Thai. They always kept me centered. Sparring especially. When I am in a position of literally getting punched in the face, everything else starts to feel small.

There were also two kinds of days: ones when we were at home researching, reading, and tinkering, and ones when we were on the road, in cars, buses, and trains, going to different spots and trying to talk to people. The road days were definitely better because they gave an illusion of momentum. It might have been false, but it kept us sane.

There were periods when we overdid the road, though, staying out all day with almost no time or energy left to soak in what was happening. Looking back, the ideal for me would be 80 percent talking and 20 percent focused digesting. We learned that soon enough and started applying it to our weeks.

Home services

I did not even know the term before September 2025. From then until the end of the year, it was something I would be obsessed with.

One observation from a little exploration was that, in San Francisco, all sorts of companies, home services or otherwise, will have already heard from startups like ours. We felt we might not be able to find enthusiastic partners. We did see this. One of the HVAC companies in San Francisco had already been contacted by a couple of other startups.

So we decided to find a spot away from Silicon Valley that would have a good density of home service companies. We felt our best bet was the East Coast, with its more extreme and varied temperatures.

We ended up getting one-way tickets to Maryland.

We tried covering a ton of spots in Maryland, more than 200, which is crazy. We used to be on the road for most of the day, then come home and distill the information. We were utterly tired after a day of driving around.

It was like a football match. Some days we went into halftime, which was lunch, without scoring anything or having a conversation that uncovered some truth. Then, suddenly, we would get one that lasted for more than an hour, and the day became a success. This was important because we were taking everything one day at a time. Our focus was just to make that day successful. It was so uncertain that we kept pushing every day and at every door, feeling, "maybe this one."

We gathered so much data doing this: the different problems these companies face, the software they use, what is good, and what is not. We religiously took notes. Sometimes the owners shared their personal stories, both good and bad, and the conversation moved from an interview to something more personal, which we did connect with.

Before moving to Maryland, we also called owners of home service businesses across the United States. First, to get information, and second, to explore the possibility of shadowing them. Conversations reveal surface-level issues, but actually working with a company for a few days is what uncovers the deeper ones, the ones that really matter and that even the owners might be looking past.

One of those calls was with Billy, one of the most genuine humans I have met, who owns a very reputable HVAC business in Florida. We had planned a ten-minute call and ended up talking for an hour and a half. At the end of it, I asked him the big question: can I come and shadow you? He said yes in no time. He said he would love new eyes on how he operates the business and suggestions for things he could make better.

This was big for us. It meant we would get a deep look at how a home service business works, from the back office to the service technicians and more.

Shadowing

In early November 2025, we landed in Orlando, Florida, and made our way to Winter Haven, also known as the land of lakes and gators.

We shadowed Billy and his team and got to live the life of a service technician every day for a week. Before this, we used to just hear a bunch of things. Now things started making a lot more sense. I started seeing how a home service company actually works.

There was huge attention to detail. The competition was intense, so small things added up. Something as simple as wearing foot covers could mean a homeowner loving your service and recommending you to others.

The software served its purpose. ServiceTitan was used religiously by everyone. But when there was not a real need to use it for things that were simply done another way, they resorted to the other way. There was monotonous work, things done repeatedly, and some of them actually enjoyed doing it.

There were also many loose threads, both in the tools the software offered and in how easy those tools were to use. One thing was clear, though: when a business owner saw something help, they were ready to pay to experiment.

We also heard about what happened when these businesses worked on big commercial projects. It took time for them to get paid, and if the finances were not handled properly, that could mean not being able to pay the service technicians and workers. On top of that, skilled labour is scarce in this industry, so the owners never wanted to lose their employees. That created enormous stress, with owners taking loans just to make payroll and hold their teams together. It was also why smaller shops resorted to smaller residential projects, where payment was immediate. There was a brief period when we explored a pivot into the lending business. Thank God we did not.

My biggest takeaway from the week, though, was Billy's leadership. He was genuinely on a mission, and he was open to feedback from two 25-year-olds who had never been in home services. He was fully aware of that, and he still wanted an outside perspective, keeping all the titles aside. He would listen to anyone and everyone about how to make his business better.

Walking away

We were having hundreds of conversations, but we still were not finding conviction in the industry. It felt as though we were forcing it on ourselves, desperate to find something non-obvious within the industry worth solving. We were forcing ourselves to find the "insight."

A lot of people talked about leads and voice calls. But we knew there were already players serving this, and we knew the market from our previous iteration in the insurance space. We did not want to participate in a lead or voice AI war.

There was not one single moment. Through December, the questions slowly increased, and we could not see a clear path forward. After we were back in San Francisco, we started taking a more formal step back.

Rock bottom

The end of 2025 was rock bottom for us. We decided to take a full break during Christmas. My good friend Vedant visited us in the Bay Area, and I got to spend good time with him and give my mind a reset. We did a road trip across the West Coast, covering about 2,500 miles. I did not touch my laptop.

After coming back, we started discussing and thinking more seriously about whether to continue.

I keep getting the question of how it makes less logical sense to keep working on a startup. I do get it. The answer I give myself is that I know at some point I want to be working toward building, and if the trough of sorrow is going to exist anyway, why not experience it right away?

I have gotten good pushback on this, and I was looking at that as well. I also started reading a book called Quit, which said that it makes perfect sense to quit at some points, and that continuing might be the riskiest thing you ever do. Like people climbing Everest who keep pushing and end up dying.

I was rethinking things. But my gut still told me to go ahead one more time and give it a shot.

The ten-year question

We gathered up the courage to give this one more try. One strict rule we set for ourselves was this: what are we going to work toward for the next ten years, even if we are not pursuing the startup?

I opened the statements of purpose I had written for my graduate school applications. I wanted to read those documents from 2020 once again. I was already thinking about healthcare and, given the position I was in, I looked to those documents for inspiration about what I wanted to do with my life.

Every application had the same line in it.

"The next decade will be an important one for the convergence of medicine and AI. I want to be at the forefront, working on these interdisciplinary challenges."

I wrote that in 2020. Here I was, asking myself what I would work toward for the next ten years, reading an answer I had already given, right in the middle of the decade I was talking about.

Looking back, I have naturally kept moving toward this space. So it was clear that this was one area that definitely held my interest. I am not saying other spaces would not interest me as much, but based on everything I knew about myself, I knew healthcare was going to remain an option.

I remembered that I had written the line. It had stayed in the back of my mind. Reading it again gave me more energy to tackle this space because it was a promise made a few years ago, with part of the decade still left to keep it.

Reading my own words from back then, they still felt true. I also felt respect for the older version of me, for aiming at that and for keeping at it.

I asked myself another question too. If I were not doing a startup, what would I be doing? My mind immediately went to either working for a health tech startup or starting something again in the space.

Back to health

Starting in mid-January 2026, we were back in health.

We started with the same way of working: going through different healthcare offices. This was my favourite period after a while of dryness. We ended up meeting very interesting people. People in healthcare are also very nice and very passionate.

We were initially exploring how we could build an EMR system from the ground up for smaller practices, so they would not need to repurpose the systems built for large companies. This felt especially relevant for concierge services, which were trying to break with the existing system by providing direct care without involving insurance.

We looked at a few different problems in this area. We first explored how we could help older adults through a single place for their care, and slowly moved through different parts of healthcare.

Shifu

One big thing we wanted this time was to be users of our own product. One thing that has stayed amazingly constant for me over the last five years is training, along with combat sports.

If you have watched Kung Fu Panda, you know Shifu, the red panda who trains Po. The word itself means teacher, the one in your corner who knows you and pushes you. That is what we want to give everyone: their own Shifu, one that can train them in any pillar they are trying to get good at. It is also why our logo is a red panda.

But we do not want to just hand everyone an AI and call it a coach. We want a Shifu that is superpowered by real experts it relies on. Behind Shifu is a care team of licensed dietitians and certified exercise coaches who review and refine what it builds for you, so what reaches you carries both the system's context and an expert's judgment.

We are starting with exercise and nutrition because they were immediate and could be tested and iterated on easily. Going forward, we want to do all of healthcare.

The way Shifu works today is that you set your goals for exercise and nutrition, and Shifu, along with licensed dietitians and certified exercise coaches, builds and adjusts your plans, working with you over text. We feel text is the easiest way for a user to interact with this kind of system, especially one that needs to be by your side every day.

We are also building the app that brings your progress, insights, and care team into one place, connected to your wearables. The app is a good place for consolidation and long-term insights.
